01.10.1 | Now, therefore, we shall expound the chapter on the Great Four Limbs (Mahācatuṣpāda).
01.10.2 | Thus indeed spoke the venerable Ātreya.
01.10.3 | Physicians speak of medicine (bheṣaja) as having four limbs and sixteen parts; what was called "sixteen-qualitied" in the previous chapter, that medicine, applied with rational planning (yukti), is sufficient for health: so said the venerable Punarvasu Ātreya.
01.10.4 | "No," said Maitreya. "What is the reason? For some patients are seen who are provided with equipment, provided with attendants and self-controlled, and attended by skilled physicians, recovering; and others, similarly endowed, dying. Therefore medicine is ineffective, just as a little water poured into a pit and into a lake, or into a flowing river, or a handful of dust scattered on a dust-heap. Likewise others are seen, without equipment, without attendants and not self-controlled, and attended by unskilled physicians, recovering; and others, similarly placed, dying."
01.10.P1 | And since one who takes counter-measures recovers, one who takes counter-measures dies; one who takes none recovers, one who takes none dies; hence it is thought that medicine is not different from non-medicine.
01.10.5 | "Maitreya, it is wrongly thought," said Ātreya. "What is the reason? What was said, that patients die though provided with this medicine endowed with sixteen qualities, is untenable; for in diseases curable by medicine, medicine is not a non-cause. And as for patients who recover without complete medicine, it is not that for them there would be no special (better) recovery from the provision of complete medicine; for just as a man raising a fallen man who is capable of rising might lend him his strength, and he would rise more quickly and without strain, so are patients through obtaining complete medicine. And as for patients who die even with complete medicine, not all of them, provided with medicine, would recover; for not all diseases are curable by means, nor is there success without the means for diseases curable by means, nor is this collection of medicine for incurable diseases; for a knowledgeable physician is not able to raise a patient who is about to die. For those who act after examination are the skilled; just as an archer who knows the technique (yoga) and is constant in practice, taking up the bow and shooting an arrow at a large target not too distant, does not miss, and accomplishes the desired task, so the physician endowed with his own qualities and equipped, having examined and beginning the action, without fault, surely brings the patient who has a curable disease to health. Therefore medicine is not indistinguishable from non-medicine."
01.10.6 | And this is evident to us: that we treat the sick with medicine that is not sick, the wasted with the not-wasted; we nourish the lean and weak, we deplete the stout and fat, we treat one overcome by heat with cold, one overcome by cold with heat; we fill up deficient dhātus, we reduce those in excess; treating diseases by reversal of their root, we properly establish them in the natural state (prakṛti). For us who act thus, this collection of medicine is the most desirable.
