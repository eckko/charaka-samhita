01.09.1 | Now, therefore, we shall expound the chapter called "Khuḍḍākacatuṣpāda" (the small chapter on the four quarters).
01.09.2 | Thus spoke the venerable Ātreya.
01.09.3a | The physician, the substances (drugs), the attendant and the patient: this set of four quarters (pāda), when endowed with qualities, is to be known as the cause of the subsiding of disorder.
01.09.3b | ~
01.09.4a | Disorder is imbalance of the dhātus; balance is called the normal state (prakṛti). Health is termed happiness, and disorder is indeed suffering.
01.09.4b | ~
01.09.5a | When the dhātus are deranged, the activity of the four, the physician and the rest, being praiseworthy, aimed at balance of the dhātus, is called treatment (cikitsā).
01.09.5b | ~
01.09.6a | Thorough clarity in learning (śruta), having seen practice many times, dexterity and purity: this is to be known as the set of four qualities in the physician.
01.09.6b | ~
01.09.7a | Abundance, suitability there, manifold modes of preparation (kalpanā), and excellence (sampad): this fourfold quality is said to belong to the substances (drugs).
01.09.7b | ~
01.09.8a | Knowledge of nursing care (upacāra), dexterity, attachment to the master, and purity: this is the fourfold quality in the attending person.
01.09.8b | ~
01.09.9a | Memory, doing as directed, fearlessness, and also the ability to describe the ailments are held to be the qualities of the patient.
01.09.9b | ~
01.09.10a | The set of four quarters, with its sixteen qualities, is the cause of success; but here the physician, as knower, prescriber and applier, is the chief.
01.09.10b | ~
01.09.11a | For just as in cooking the vessel, fuel and fire are the means of the cook, and in conquest the ground, the army and weapons are those of the conqueror,
01.09.11b | ~
01.09.12a | so in success the quarters beginning with the patient are termed the means of the physician; hence in treatment the chief cause is the physician.
01.09.12b | ~
01.09.13a | Just as clay, stick, wheel, string and the like bring about no benefit (guṇa) without the potter, so the three quarters bring about none without the physician.
01.09.13b | ~
01.09.14a | That very dreadful disorders vanish like a city of the Gandharvas, and that others, awaiting the remedy, quickly grow —
01.09.14b | ~
01.09.15a | When the three quarters are present, the knowing and the ignorant physician are the cause here. Better that the ignorant one offer up his own self as an oblation than that he undertake treatment.
01.09.15b | ~
01.09.16a | Like one without eyes going by groping with his hands, as if in constant fear, like a boat at the mercy of the wind, the ignorant physician, through lack of knowledge, moves about in his procedures.
01.09.16b | ~
01.09.17a | Having by chance brought through one who has come to him with a fixed life-span, the one who fancies himself a physician quickly kills hundreds of those whose life-span is not fixed.
01.09.17b | ~
01.09.18a | Therefore the physician accomplished in the four, namely the treatise (śāstra), the understanding of its meaning, practice, and observation of procedures, is called a restorer of life (prāṇābhisara).
01.09.18b | ~
01.09.19a | He whose knowledge is fourfold, of the cause, the sign, the alleviation and the non-recurrence of diseases, is the best of physicians, worthy of a king.
01.09.19b | ~
01.09.20a | A weapon, treatises and water depend on the recipient (pātra) for turning to merit or to fault; hence one should purify one's understanding for the sake of treatment.
01.09.20b | ~
01.09.21a | Learning, reasoning, specialised knowledge (vijñāna), memory, devotion to the work, and practice: for him who has these six qualities, nothing curable passes beyond his reach.
01.09.21b | ~
01.09.22a | Learning, intellect, observation of practice, repeated practice, success and support (āśraya): even each single one of these is enough for earning the title 'vaidya' (physician).
01.09.22b | ~
01.09.23a | But he who has all these good qualities, learning and the rest, deserves the title 'vaidya' in its true sense, a giver of happiness to living beings.
01.09.23b | ~
01.09.24a | The treatise is a light for illumination; one's own intellect is the sight. Treating with these two well combined, the physician does not err.
01.09.24b | ~
01.09.25a | Since in treatment the three quarters depend on the physician, the physician should strive for the excellence of his own qualities.
01.09.25b | ~
01.09.26a | Friendliness, compassion towards the afflicted, interest in the curable (śakya), and disregard towards beings abiding in their natural state (prakṛtistha): this is the physician's fourfold conduct.
01.09.26b | ~
01.09.27a | Here, the two verses: that treatment (bhiṣagjita) has four quarters; that each quarter has four qualities; why the physician is the chief among the quarters; and of what qualities the physician is;
01.09.27b | ~
01.09.27c | ~
01.09.28a | the kinds of knowledge, and the fourfold Brāhmī disposition (buddhi) of physicians: all this has been set forth in the Khuḍḍāka Catuṣpāda.
01.09.28b | ~
01.09.P1 | Thus, in the treatise composed by Agniveśa and redacted by Caraka, in the Śloka section (Ślokasthāna), the ninth chapter, named Khuḍḍākacatuṣpāda.
