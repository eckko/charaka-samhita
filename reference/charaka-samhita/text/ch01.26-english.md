01.26.2 | Thus said the venerable Ātreya.

01.26.6a | these great sages, advanced in learning and age and self-controlled, came together in the delightful Caitraratha forest, wishing to stroll about.
01.26.6b | ~
01.26.7a | Among them, the knowers of meaning, seated there, this meaningful discussion arose on the proper determination of taste and food.
01.26.7b | ~
01.26.8 | "Taste is one alone," said Bhadrakāpya, "the entity which the skilled call one of the five sense-objects, belonging to the domain of the tongue; and that, again, is not other than water."
01.26.P1 | "There are two tastes," (said) the brāhmaṇa Śākunteya, "the cutting (chedanīya) and the pacifying (upaśamanīya)."
01.26.P2 | "There are three tastes," (said) Pūrṇākṣa Maudgalya, "the cutting, the pacifying and the common (sādhāraṇa)."
01.26.P3 | "There are four tastes," (said) Hiraṇyākṣa Kauśika, "the palatable and wholesome, the palatable and unwholesome, the unpalatable and wholesome, and the unpalatable and unwholesome."
01.26.P4 | "There are five tastes," (said) Kumāraśiras Bharadvāja, "the earthy, the watery, the fiery, the airy and the ethereal (of the sky)." "There are six tastes," (said) the royal sage Vāryovida, "the heavy, the light, the cold, the hot, the unctuous and the dry."
01.26.P5 | "There are seven tastes," (said) Nimi of Videha, "sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, astringent and alkali (kṣāra)."
01.26.P6 | "There are eight tastes," (said) Baḍiśa Dhāmārgava, "sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter, astringent, alkali and the unmanifest (avyakta)."
01.26.P7 | "The tastes are countless," (said) Kāṅkāyana, the physician of Bāhlīka, "because the particulars of the substrata (āśraya), qualities, actions and savour (saṃsvāda) are countless."
01.26.9 | "The tastes are six alone," said the venerable Punarvasu Ātreya, "sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent."
01.26.P8 | Of these six tastes the source is water. Cutting and pacifying are the two actions; from the mixing of the two comes commonness (sādhāraṇatva). Palatability and unpalatability are (a matter of) liking (bhakti); wholesome and unwholesome are the effects (prabhāva). The substrata (āśraya), however, are modifications of the five great elements, dependent on nature, modification, vicāra, place and time. In these substrata, called substances (dravya), are the qualities (guṇa) heavy, light, cold, hot, unctuous, dry and the rest. Alkali (kṣāra) is so called from wearing away (kṣaraṇa); it is not a taste: it is a substance, produced from many tastes, having many tastes, mostly pungent and salty, accompanied by many sense-objects, produced by a process (karaṇa). Unmanifestness of the tastes, however, occurs in their source (prakṛti), in the subsidiary taste (anurasa), or in a substance accompanied by a subsidiary taste. Again, countlessness (of the tastes) because the particulars of those entities, the substrata and the rest, are countless is not proper: for each one of these entities, the substrata and the rest, takes on particulars, since particulars are countless, and otherness (of taste) does not follow from that, because they are for the most part combined with one another; nor does countlessness arise, from their production, for these (tastes) whose nature is qualities. Therefore the intelligent do not teach the action of combined tastes.
01.26.P9 | And with that very reason in view, we shall teach the separate characteristics of the six tastes not combined with one another.
01.26.10 | But first, for now, we shall say something with regard to the division of substances.
01.26.P10 | In this context every substance is made of the five elements (pāñcabhautika); it is sentient and insentient. Its qualities are sound and the rest, and heavy and the rest ending with fluid (drava); action is stated to be fivefold, emesis and the rest.
01.26.11 | Among them, substances abounding in the qualities heavy, rough, hard, slow, stable, non-slimy, dense, gross and smell are earthy (pārthiva); they produce increase (upacaya), compactness, heaviness and stability. Those abounding in the qualities fluid, unctuous, cold, slow, soft, slimy and taste are watery (āpya); they produce moistening (upakleda), unctuousness, binding, liquefaction (viṣyanda), softness and refreshment. Those abounding in the qualities hot, sharp, subtle, light, dry, non-slimy and form (rūpa) are fiery (āgneya); they produce burning, pāka (cooking, digestion), lustre, light and colour. Those abounding in the qualities light, cold, dry, rough, non-slimy, subtle and touch are airy (vāyavya); they produce dryness, weariness (glāni), movement (vicāra), non-sliminess and lightness. Those abounding in the qualities soft, light, subtle, smooth and sound are of the nature of space (ākāśa); they produce softness, porosity and lightness.
01.26.12 | By this teaching, no substance is found in the world that is not a medicine, with regard to this or that rational planning (yukti) and this or that purpose.
01.26.13 | But substances are not effective through the power (prabhāva) of their qualities alone; for what substances do, through the power of the substance, through the power of the qualities, and through the power of substance and qualities, at this or that time, having reached this or that site, with regard to this or that rational planning (yukti) and this or that purpose, that is action (karma); that by which they do it is potency (vīrya); where they do it is the site (adhikaraṇa); when they do it is the time (kāla); how they do it is the means (upāya); what they accomplish is the result (phala).

01.26.34b | ~
01.26.35a | Thus all the qualities beginning with para have been stated with their own characteristics; when they are not known, treatment does not proceed properly.
01.26.35b | ~
01.26.36a | Qualities are not said to have qualities as their support; therefore the physician should know the qualities of the tastes as the qualities of the substances. The intentions of the author are of various kinds.
01.26.36b | ~
01.26.37a | And therefore, having understood the matter in hand, the differences of place and time, the intentions of the author of the treatise and the means, one should declare the meaning.
01.26.37b | ~
01.26.38a | Next after this I shall declare the six divisions of the tastes, and how the six tastes, arising from the five elements, are enumerated.
01.26.38b | ~
01.26.39 | The waters are indeed saumya (of the nature of soma), arising in the atmosphere, cold by nature, light and of unmanifest taste; while falling from the atmosphere and having fallen, endowed with the qualities of the five great elements, they nourish the bodies of mobile and immobile beings; in those bodies the six tastes take shape.
01.26.40 | Of these six tastes, the sweet taste comes from excess of the quality of soma; the sour from predominance of earth and fire; the salty from predominance of water and fire; the pungent from predominance of air and fire; the bitter from excess of air and space (ākāśa); and the astringent from excess of air (pavana) and earth.
01.26.P11 | Thus the sixfoldness of these tastes is established through the particular deficiency and excess of the great elements, just like the various particular colours and forms of immobile and mobile beings; and because time has six seasons, the particular deficiency and excess of the great elements is established.
01.26.41 | Among them, the tastes made of fire and air (māruta) mostly tend upward, owing to the lightness and upward floating of air and the upward flaming of fire; those made of water and earth mostly tend downward, owing to the heaviness of earth and the downward course of water; and those of mixed nature tend both ways.
01.26.42 | We shall explain the qualities and actions of each one of these six tastes according to the substance.
01.26.43.1 | Among them, the sweet taste, because of its suitability to the body, increases rasa, blood, flesh, fat, bone, marrow, ojas (vital essence) and semen; it promotes life, clears the six sense faculties, produces strength and complexion, destroys Pitta, poison and Vāta (māruta), allays thirst and burning, is good for the skin, the hair and the throat, is strengthening, gladdening, vitalizing, replenishing (tarpaṇa) and nourishing, gives firmness, unites the wasted and the wounded, delights the nose, mouth, throat, lips and tongue, allays burning and mūrcchā (fainting), is most liked by bees and ants, and is unctuous, cold and heavy.
01.26.P12 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively it produces corpulence (sthaulya), softness, lassitude, excessive sleep, heaviness, lack of desire for food, weakness of the digestive fire, growth of flesh in the mouth and throat, and Kapha-born disorders such as dyspnoea, cough, coryza (pratiśyāya), alasaka, cold fever, distension (ānāha), sweetness of the mouth, vomiting (vamathu), loss of consciousness and of voice, galagaṇḍa, gaṇḍamālā, ślīpada, swelling of the throat, coating of the bladder, vessels and throat, eye disease and abhiṣyanda.
01.26.43.2 | The sour taste makes food relished, kindles the digestive fire (agni), nourishes the body, invigorates, awakens the mind, makes the senses firm, increases strength, moves Vāta downward, refreshes the heart, makes the mouth water, moves down what has been eaten, moistens, digests, gladdens, and is light, hot and unctuous.
01.26.P13 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively it sets the teeth on edge, causes thirst, makes the eyes close, makes the body hairs bristle, liquefies Kapha, increases Pitta, vitiates the blood, burns the flesh, makes the body lax, and brings about swelling (śvayathu) in the wasted, wounded, lean and weak; moreover, because of its fiery nature, it causes pāka (inflammation) in what is wounded, struck, bitten, burnt, broken, swollen, dislocated, urinated upon, crept over, crushed, cut, split, separated, pierced, pounded and the like, and it burns the throat, chest and heart.
01.26.43.3 | The salty taste is digesting, moistening, stimulating digestion, dislodging, cutting, breaking, sharp, flowing, spreading, moving downward, making space, Vāta-removing, dispersing stiffness, binding and compaction, and opposed to all the tastes; it makes the mouth water, liquefies Kapha, cleanses the passages, softens all the parts of the body, makes food relished, goes with food, and is not excessively heavy, and is unctuous and hot.
01.26.P14 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively it aggravates Pitta, increases the blood, causes thirst, causes mūrcchā, heats, causes cracking, tears, makes the flesh ooze away, increases kuṣṭhas and poison, bursts swellings, makes the teeth fall, impairs virility, obstructs the senses, and brings about wrinkles, grey hair and baldness; moreover, it produces disorders such as lohitapitta, amlapitta, vīsarpa, vātarakta, vicarcikā and indralupta.
01.26.43.4 | The pungent taste cleanses the mouth, kindles the digestive fire, dries what has been eaten, makes the nose discharge, makes the eyes discharge, makes the senses clear, destroys alasaka, swelling (śvayathu), increase of body (upacaya), udarda, abhiṣyanda, fat (sneha), sweat, moistness (kleda) and wastes, makes food relished, destroys itching, makes wounds subside, kills worms (krimi), scrapes the flesh, breaks up clotted blood, cuts bindings, opens the passages, pacifies Kapha (śleṣman), and is light, hot and dry.
01.26.P15 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively it impairs virility by the power of its vipāka; by the power of its taste and vīrya it stupefies, exhausts, depresses, emaciates, causes mūrcchā, bends (the body), causes darkness of sight, causes giddiness, burns the throat, produces heat of the body, diminishes strength and produces thirst; moreover, from the abundance of the qualities of air and fire, it produces Vāta-born (māruta) disorders, with giddiness, burning (davathu), tremor, pricking and splitting pain, in the feet, arms, sides, back and so on.
01.26.43.5 | The bitter taste, though itself unrelished, destroys arocaka (lack of relish); it destroys poison and worms (krimi), allays mūrcchā, burning, itching, kuṣṭha and thirst, makes the skin and flesh firm, destroys fever, stimulates digestion, digests, purifies breast milk (stanya), scrapes (lekhana), dries up moistness (kleda), fat, muscle-fat (vasā), marrow, lasīkā (watery body fluid), pus, sweat, urine, faeces, Pitta and Kapha (śleṣman), and is dry, cold and light.
01.26.P16 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively, because of its dryness and its rough and non-slimy nature, it dries up rasa, blood, flesh, fat, bone, marrow and semen, brings about roughness of the channels, takes away strength, emaciates, exhausts, stupefies, causes giddiness, dries the mouth, and produces other Vāta disorders.
01.26.43.6 | The astringent taste is pacifying, binding, uniting, compressing, healing, drying, checking, allays Kapha (śleṣman), blood and Pitta, consumes the moistness (kleda) of the body, and is dry, cold and not light.
01.26.P17 | Though it has such qualities, when used alone and excessively it dries the mouth, afflicts the heart, distends the abdomen, restrains speech, obstructs the channels, brings about duskiness, impairs virility, is digested with stagnation, retains wind, urine, faeces and semen, emaciates, exhausts, causes thirst, causes stiffness, and, because of its roughness, non-sliminess and dryness, produces Vāta disorders such as pakṣavadha (striking down of one side), graha (seizing), apatānaka and ardita.
01.26.44 | Thus these six tastes, used separately or in combination, properly and in due measure, are for the benefit of the world of embodied beings (adhyātmaloka); but used otherwise than this, they become harmful. The wise one should use them properly and in due measure, only for benefit.

01.26.80 | To the venerable Ātreya, who had spoken thus, Agniveśa said: 'Venerable one, this true speech of the venerable one, endowed with excellence of meaning, (given) properly on the topic of substances, qualities and actions, has been heard. But next we wish to hear the characteristics of the incompatible (vairodhika) food articles (āhāravikāra) taught not too briefly.'
01.26.81 | The venerable Ātreya said to him: Substances that are opposed to the body-dhātus enter into conflict with the body-dhātus: some are opposed through their mutual qualities, some through combination, others through processing, and others by place, time, measure and the like, and likewise others by their own nature.
01.26.82 | Among these, of the (substances) which are used most with reference to food, we shall teach a part, with reference to the incompatible. One should not eat fish together with milk. For both of these are sweet, sweet in vipāka and greatly channel-clogging (abhiṣyandin); because of being cold and hot they are opposed in vīrya; because of being opposed in vīrya they lead to vitiation of the blood, and because of being greatly channel-clogging, to obstruction of the passages (mārga).
01.26.83 | Having heard that statement of Ātreya, Bhadrakāpya then said to Agniveśa: 'One may eat all fish indeed with milk, except the one cilicima. That one, moreover, is scaly, red-eyed, with red streaks all over and shaped like the rohita, and it mostly moves on the ground. 01.26.84 | 'No,' said the venerable Ātreya; 'one should not eat any fish at all with milk, but especially not the cilicima; for it, because of being greatly channel-clogging, produces these diseases with grosser signs and stirs up āmaviṣa (poison of undigested matter).'
01.26.P18 | And one should not eat the meats of domestic, marsh and aquatic (animals) together with honey, sesame, jaggery, milk, māṣa, mūlaka, lotus stalk or sprouted grains; for, caused by that, one gets deafness, blindness, tremor, torpor (jāḍya), indistinct speech (kala), dumbness, nasal speech (maiṇmiṇya), or death.
01.26.P19 | One should not eat the pauṣkara or rohiṇīka vegetable, or pigeons fried in mustard oil, together with honey and milk; for, caused by that, one gets one of these: clogging of the blood (śoṇitābhiṣyanda), accumulation in the vessels (dhamanīpravicaya), apasmāra, śaṅkhaka, galagaṇḍa and rohiṇī, or death.
01.26.P20 | Having eaten mūlaka, laśuna, kṛṣṇagandhā, arjaka, sumukha, surasa and the like, one should not take milk, for fear of affliction by kuṣṭha.
01.26.P21 | Neither the jātuka vegetable nor ripe nikuca should be taken together with honey and milk; for this leads to death, or to obstruction of strength, complexion, lustre and virility (vīrya), to serious disease, and to impotence.
01.26.P22 | That same ripe nikuca should not be taken with māṣa soup, jaggery and ghee, because of incompatibility. Likewise āmra, āmrātaka, mātuluṅga, nikuca, karamarda, moca, dantaśaṭha, badara, kośāmra, bhavya, jāmbava, kapittha, tintiḍīka, pārāvata, akṣoḍa, panasa, nālikera, dāḍima and āmalaka, and other substances of this kind, and everything sour, liquid or non-liquid, is incompatible with milk.
01.26.P23 | Likewise kaṅgu, vanaka, makuṣṭhaka, kulattha, māṣa and niṣyāva are incompatible with milk.
01.26.P24 | The padmottarikā vegetable, śārkara, maireya and honey, used together, are incompatible and greatly aggravate Vāta.
01.26.P25 | Hāridraka fried in mustard oil is incompatible and greatly aggravates Pitta.
01.26.P26 | Milk-rice (pāyasa) with mantha as the after-drink is incompatible and greatly aggravates Kapha (śleṣman).
01.26.P27 | Upodikā cooked with sesame paste is a cause of diarrhoea (atīsāra).
01.26.P28 | The balākā is incompatible with vāruṇī, and also with kulmāṣa; that same (balākā), fried in pig's muscle-fat, kills at once.
01.26.P29 | Peacock meat fixed on a castor (eraṇḍa) sīsaka, scorched on a castor fire and combined with castor oil, kills at once.
01.26.P30 | Hāridraka meat fixed on a hāridra sīsaka and scorched on a hāridra fire kills at once; that same (meat), covered with ash and dust and taken with honey, is for immediate death.
01.26.P31 | Long peppers (pippalī) prepared in the frying of fish (matsyanistālana), and likewise kākamācī and honey (together), lead to death.
01.26.P32 | Hot honey, and honey for one afflicted by heat, lead to death.
01.26.P33 | Honey and ghee in equal measure; honey and rain-water in equal measure; honey and lotus seed (puṣkarabīja); hot water after drinking honey; bhallātaka with hot water; kampillaka prepared in buttermilk; stale kākamācī; and bhāsa roasted on a spit over charcoal: these are incompatible.
