01.11.1 | Now, therefore, we shall expound the chapter on the Three Pursuits (Tisraiṣaṇīya).
01.11.2 | Thus indeed spoke the venerable Ātreya.
01.11.3 | Here indeed, three pursuits (eṣaṇā) are to be pursued by a man whose mind (sattva), intellect, manliness and vigour are unimpaired and who looks to his welfare in this world and in the other.
01.11.P1 | Namely: the pursuit of life, the pursuit of wealth, and the pursuit of the other world.
01.11.4 | Of these pursuits, one should first of all take up the pursuit of life.
01.11.P2 | Why? Because in giving up life, everything is given up.
01.11.P3 | Its preservation is this: for the healthy, following the regimen of the healthy (svasthavṛtta); for the patient, carefulness in allaying the disorder. Both of these have been told and will be told. Following that as told, one obtains a long life through preserving life. Thus the first pursuit is explained.
01.11.5 | Then one should take up the second, the pursuit of wealth; for immediately after life, wealth indeed is to be sought. For there is nothing more evil than this evil: a long life for one who has no means of living (upakaraṇa). Therefore one should strive to seek the means of living.
01.11.P4 | On that we shall explain the ways of gaining the means of living, namely: farming, cattle-keeping, trade, service of the king and the like; and whatever other occupations, not condemned by the good and producing livelihood and prosperity, one may know, those one should undertake to do. Doing so, a man lives a long life and is not despised.
01.11.P5 | Thus the second, the pursuit of wealth, is explained.
01.11.6 | Then one should take up the third, the pursuit of the other world.
01.11.P6 | And here there is doubt: 'Having departed from here, shall we exist or not?' But whence the doubt? It is said: there are some, devoted to perception (pratyakṣa), who because rebirth is imperceptible have resorted to disbelief (nāstikya); and there are others who accept rebirth only from faith in scripture (āgama); and from the difference of traditions (śruti): 'Some consider mother and father the cause of birth,
01.11.P7 | others, nature (svabhāva), creation by another, and chance (yadṛcchā).' Hence the doubt: is there indeed rebirth or not?
01.11.7 | In that matter the intelligent person should give up the notion of disbelief and doubt.
01.11.P8 | Why? For the perceptible is little; much is the imperceptible, which is apprehended by scripture (āgama), inference (anumāna) and reasoning (yukti). The very sense organs by which the perceptible is apprehended are themselves imperceptible.
01.11.8 | And existing forms are not apprehended by perception because of excessive nearness, excessive distance, being covered, weakness of the sense organ, instability of the mind, mixture with similar things, being overpowered, and extreme subtlety. Therefore this is said without examination: 'Only the perceptible exists; nothing else exists.'
01.11.9a | And these traditions are not an authority, because they conflict with reasoning (yukti). If the self of the mother or of the father were to pass into the offspring, the self would pass in one of two ways: either whole or by a part.

01.11.9c | ~
01.11.10a | If it passed whole, the death of the mother or father would occur; and of the subtle self there is no part at all (nirantaram).
01.11.10b | ~
01.11.11a | Intellect and mind are determined to be just as the self is. And for those who hold this view, there is no fourfold womb (yoni).
01.11.11b | ~
01.11.12a | One should know the own characteristic of the six dhātus to be natural (svābhāvika); but in their union and separation, action (karma) alone is the cause.
01.11.12b | ~
01.11.13a | Creation by another is not accepted for the beginningless conscious dhātu. If that 'other' is the self and it is accepted as the cause, then let there be creation by another.
01.11.13b | ~
01.11.14a | There is neither examination nor anything to be examined, neither agent nor cause; neither gods nor sages nor siddhas (perfected ones), neither action nor the fruit of action.
01.11.14b | ~
01.11.15a | For the disbeliever (nāstika), whose self is struck down by chance (yadṛcchā), there is no self at all; and this clinging to disbelief is a sin beyond sins.
01.11.15b | ~
01.11.16a | Therefore the wise person, giving up this view that has gone along a wrong path, should see everything as it truly is by the lamp of the intellect of the good.
01.11.16b | ~
01.11.17 | Everything is indeed twofold: existent (sat) and non-existent (asat). Its examination is fourfold: the teaching of the reliable (āptopadeśa), perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna) and reasoning (yukti).
01.11.18a | First, the reliable (āpta): those who by the power of austerity and knowledge are freed from rajas and tamas, whose knowledge of the three times is stainless and always unobstructed,

01.11.18c | ~
01.11.19a | they are the reliable, the cultured (śiṣṭa), the awakened; their word is beyond doubt. They will speak the truth; why would they, free from rajas and tamas, speak untruth?
01.11.19b | ~
01.11.20a | The cognition (buddhi) which arises from the contact of self, sense organs, mind and objects, and is clear at that very moment, is called perception (pratyakṣa).
01.11.20b | ~
01.11.21a | What is preceded by perception, threefold and of the three times, is inferred: hidden fire by smoke, sexual union from seeing pregnancy;
01.11.21b | ~
01.11.22a | in this way the wise determine the past; and, having seen here a similar fruit born from a seed, (they determine) the future fruit from a seed.
01.11.22b | ~
01.11.23a | From the combination of water, ploughing, seed and season comes the growth of crops: this is reasoning (yukti); likewise, from the combination of the six dhātus comes the origin of embryos.
01.11.23b | ~
01.11.24a | From the combination of the wood to be churned (mathya), the churning (manthana) and the churning stick (manthāna) comes the origin of fire; the excellence of the four limbs (catuṣpāda), joined with reasoning (yukti), removes disease.
01.11.24b | ~
01.11.25a | The intellect which sees things arising from the combination of many causes is to be known as reasoning (yukti), extending over the three times, by which the triad (trivarga) is accomplished.
01.11.25b | ~
01.11.26a | This is the examination (there is no other) by which everything is examined. What is to be examined is thus the existent and the non-existent; and by it, rebirth exists.
01.11.26b | ~
01.11.27 | Among these, reliable scripture (āptāgama) is first the Veda; and any other statement of a treatise (śāstravāda) which is not contrary to the meaning of the Veda, is composed by examiners, approved by the cultured and set forth for the benefit of the world, that too is reliable scripture. From reliable scripture it is learnt that charity, austerity, sacrifice, truth, non-violence and celibacy produce prosperity (abhyudaya) and the highest good (niḥśreyasa).
01.11.28 | And for those who have not passed beyond the faults of the mind, non-rebirth (apunarbhava) is not taught in the gateways of dharma by the faultless.
01.11.29 | And one should thus determine that rebirth has been taught, after they had seen it, by the former and still earlier great sages of divine vision, who were attentive to the gateways of dharma, from whom fear, attachment, hatred, greed, delusion and pride had gone, devoted to brahman, reliable, knowers of action, and whose working of mind and intellect was unimpaired.
01.11.30 | And by perception too it is found: offspring unlike their mother and father; among those of equal origin, differences of complexion, voice, form, mind, intellect and fortune; birth in a high or low family; servitude and lordship; happiness and unhappiness; life-span; inequality of life-span; obtaining here what was not done; among the untaught, the occurrence of crying, sucking the breast, laughing, fear and the like; the appearance of marks (lakṣaṇa); difference of result where actions are alike; intelligence in one activity and lack of intelligence in another; memory of former birth, that is, the coming here of those who departed from here; and being liked or disliked where appearance is equal.
01.11.31 | Just from this it is inferred that this is the fruit of that action (karma) which is done by oneself, unavoidable, imperishable, belonging to the former body, called daiva (fate) and carried along; and that from this another will come. From the fruit the seed is inferred, and the fruit from the seed.
01.11.32 | And this is the reasoning (yukti): from the assembly of the six dhātus, the birth of the embryo; from the union of agent and instrument, action; there is fruit of action done, not of what is not done; no sprout arises without a seed; the fruit resembles the action; one thing does not arise from the seed of another. Such is the reasoning.
01.11.33 | Thus, rebirth having been taught by the four means of valid knowledge (pramāṇa), one should be attentive to the gateways of dharma, namely: to service of the teacher, study, observance of vows, taking a wife, begetting offspring, supporting dependants, honouring guests, charity, non-covetousness, austerity, freedom from envy, blameless bodily, verbal and mental action, examination of body, sense organs, mind, objects, intellect and self, and concentration of the mind; and whatever other actions of this kind, not condemned by the good, leading to heaven and producing livelihood and prosperity, one may know, those one should undertake to do. Doing so, one gains fame here indeed, and heaven after death.
01.11.P9 | Thus the third, the pursuit of the other world, is explained.
01.11.34 | Now indeed there are three supports, three kinds of strength, three sources (āyatana), three diseases, three pathways of disease, three kinds of physicians and three kinds of medicine.
01.11.35 | "Three supports" means: food, sleep and continence (brahmacarya). Supported by these three supports, employed with rational planning (yukti), the body continues, endowed with strength, complexion and growth, up to the limit of the conditioning of its life-span, in one who does not resort to a harmful regimen (saṃskāra), which will be taught right here.
01.11.36 | "Three kinds of strength" means: innate (sahaja), time-born (kālaja) and acquired through rational planning (yuktikṛta).
01.11.P10 | The innate is that which is natural to body and mind (sattva); the time-made is that born of the division of the seasons and that made by age; the acquired, again, is that which is born of the application (yoga) of food and activity.
01.11.37 | "Three sources" means: excessive use (atiyoga), non-use (ayoga) and wrong use (mithyāyoga) of the objects (artha), of action (karma) and of time (kāla).
01.11.P11 | Of these, excessive seeing of very brilliant visible objects is excessive use (atiyoga); not seeing at all is non-use (ayoga); seeing forms that are too near, too far, fierce, terrifying, astonishing, hated, loathsome, deformed, frightening and the like is wrong use (mithyāyoga). Likewise, excessive hearing of excessive sounds such as thunder, drums and shrieks is excessive use; not hearing at all is non-use; hearing sounds that are harsh, or of the loss of the dear, of injury, of assault, of terror and the like is wrong use. Likewise, excessive smelling of very sharp, strong and channel-clogging (abhiṣyandin) smells is excessive use; not smelling at all is non-use; smelling putrid, disliked, impure and rotten smells, poisonous air, the smell of corpses and the like is wrong use. Likewise, with tastes, excessive taking is excessive use; not taking at all is non-use; wrong use will be taught in the sources of the special rules of diet (āhāravidhiviśeṣāyatana), excluding quantity (rāśi). Likewise, excessive resort to very cold and hot tangible objects and to bathing, oil massage, rubbing with unguents and the like is excessive use; not resorting to them at all is non-use; resort to bathing and the like and to tangible objects cold, hot and the like without proper sequence, and uneven positions, blows, contact with impure things and with bhūtas, and the like, are wrong use.
01.11.38 | Of these, the one sense of touch (sparśana) pervades the senses and is inherent (samavāyin) in the mind; and through the pervasion of touch the mind too is pervasive. Therefore the particular state produced by pervasive touch in all the senses is, by reason of unsuitability (anupaśaya), the unwholesome contact of the senses with their objects (asātmyendriyārthasaṃyoga), five-fold and with three gradations; for "suitable" (sātmya) means "agreeable" (upaśaya).
01.11.39 | Action (karma) is the activity of speech, mind and body.
01.11.P12 | Of these, excessive activity of speech, mind and body is excessive use; complete inactivity is non-use; suppressing and forcing the urges (vega), stumbling and falling on uneven ground, wrong positioning of the limbs, defiling the limbs, striking, crushing, obstructing the breath, tormenting and the like is the bodily wrong use; tale-bearing, lying, untimely, quarrelsome, unpleasant, incoherent, discourteous and harsh speech and the like is the wrong use of speech; fear, grief, anger, greed, delusion, pride, envy, wrong views and the like is the mental wrong use.
01.11.40 | And in brief, any harmful action born of speech, mind and body, apart from excessive use and non-use, that has not been mentioned: that too one should know as wrong use.
01.11.41 | Thus one should determine that action, three-fold indeed and with three gradations, is offence of intellect (prajñāparādha).
01.11.42 | And Hemanta, Grīṣma and Varṣā, marked by cold, heat and rain, are the year (saṃvatsara); that is time (kāla).
01.11.P13 | Of these, a season (kāla) with its own features in excess is excessive use of time (kālātiyoga); one with its own features deficient is non-use of time (kālāyoga); but one with features opposite to its own proper features is wrong use of time (kālamithyāyoga).
01.11.P14 | Time, again, is called transformation (pariṇāma).
01.11.43 | Thus unwholesome contact of the senses with objects, offence of intellect and transformation: these three, with three gradations, are the causes of disorders (vikāra); but joined with balanced use (samayoga) they become causes of the normal state.
01.11.44 | Indeed, of all entities, existence and non-existence are not perceived without proper use (yoga), non-use, excessive use and wrong use; for existence and non-existence depend on their respective application (yukti).
01.11.45 | "Three diseases" means: endogenous (nija), exogenous (āgantu) and mental (mānasa).
01.11.P15 | Of these, the endogenous arises from the bodily doṣas; the exogenous arises from bhūtas, poison, wind, fire, blows and the like; the mental, again, arises from not gaining the desired and from gaining the undesired.
01.11.46 | In that case the intelligent man, even when seized by mental disease, should, examining again and again with his intellect what is wholesome and unwholesome, strive not to resort to harmful dharma, wealth (artha) and pleasure (kāma), and to resort to the beneficial; for without this triad nothing mental comes about in the world, whether happiness or sorrow. Therefore this too is to be practised: one should strive to attend on those versed in it, and to know properly oneself, place, family, time, strength and capacity.
01.11.47a | And here there is: For what is mental, the remedy is careful consideration of the triad (trivarga), attendance on those versed in it, and knowledge of oneself and the rest in every respect.

01.11.48 | "Three pathways of disease" means: the periphery (śākhā), the vital points (marma) and bone-joints, and the trunk (koṣṭha).
01.11.P16 | Of these, the periphery (śākhā) is the dhātus beginning with blood, and the skin; that is the external pathway of disease. The vital points (marma) are the bladder, heart, head and the like; the bone-joints are the unions of bones, and the ligaments and tendons attached there; that is the middle pathway of disease. The trunk (koṣṭha) is called in the treatise by the synonyms "great channel", "middle of the body", "great hollow" and "āmāśaya and pakvāśaya"; that is the internal pathway of disease.
01.11.49 | Of these, gaṇḍa, piḍakā, alajī, apacī, carmakīla, adhimāṃsa, maṣaka, kuṣṭha, vyaṅga and similar disorders, and visarpa, swelling (śvayathu), gulma, arśas, vidradhi and the like born in the external pathway, are diseases that follow the periphery (śākhā); pakṣavadha, graha, apatānaka, ardita, śoṣa, rājayakṣman, pain in the bone-joints, gudabhraṃśa and the like, and diseases of the head, heart and bladder and the like, are diseases that follow the middle pathway; fever (jvara), diarrhoea (atīsāra), vomiting (chardi), alasaka, visūcikā, cough (kāsa), dyspnoea (śvāsa), hiccup (hikkā), ānāha, udara, spleen disorders (plīhan) and the like, and visarpa, swelling, gulma, arśas, vidradhi and the like born in the internal pathway, are diseases that follow the trunk (koṣṭha).

01.11.55 | Indeed, in aggravation of the bodily doṣas, relying on the body itself, they hold medicine to be mostly of three kinds: internal cleansing (antaḥparimārjana), external cleansing (bahiḥparimārjana) and application of the knife (śastrapraṇidhāna).
01.11.P18 | Of these, internal cleansing is the medicine that enters the interior of the body and wipes away diseases born of food; that which, relying on external contact, wipes away diseases by oil massage (abhyaṅga), sweating (sveda), plaster (pradeha), affusion (pariṣeka), kneading (unmardana) and the like is external cleansing; application of the knife, again, is excision, incision, puncturing, splitting, scraping, extraction, scarification, suturing, probing, alkali (kṣāra) and leeches.
